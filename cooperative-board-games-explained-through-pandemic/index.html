Cooperative Board Games Explained Through Pandemic

How cooperative board games differ from competitive ones, using the official Pandemic rulebook to explain shared win and loss conditions.

Board Game NightsOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Most classic board games pit players against each other. Cooperative games flip that structure entirely: everyone is on the same team, working against the game itself. Pandemic, designed by Matt Leacock and published by Z-Man Games, is one of the clearest examples of this format, and its official rulebook spells out exactly how a cooperative win-or-lose condition works.

The core idea: you all win or you all lose

The Pandemic rulebook states this directly: "Pandemic is a cooperative game. The players all win or lose together." There is no individual scoring, no player who can "win" while others lose. This single sentence is the most important thing to explain to a group playing a cooperative game for the first time, because it changes how people should talk during the game. Strategy discussion and shared planning aren't just allowed, they're the entire point, unlike in a competitive game where revealing your plan to another player might hurt you.

How you win

The shared goal in Pandemic is to discover cures for all four diseases, represented by colored cubes on a world map. The rulebook describes the cure action precisely: "At any research station, discard 5 City cards of the same disease color to cure that disease." One role, the Scientist, has a documented advantage here: that role "need[s] only 4 cards of the same color to do the Discover a Cure action" instead of 5, making the Scientist especially valuable for being assigned to handle a particular disease color.

How you lose, and there are three separate ways

The rulebook lists three distinct loss conditions, and all three matter for how the game feels tense even though everyone is cooperating:

  1. 8 outbreaks occur (described in the rules as "a worldwide panic happens").
  2. Not enough disease cubes are left when needed ("a disease spreads too much").
  3. Not enough player cards are left when needed ("your team runs out of time").

Having three separate failure conditions, rather than one, means a cooperative game can create tension even without any player competing against another. The group has to simultaneously manage disease spread, card supply, and outbreak count, and losing track of any single one of the three can end the game regardless of how well the other two are going.

The turn structure

Each player's turn follows a fixed three-step order, according to the rulebook's own turn summary: "1. Do 4 actions 2. Draw 2 cards (resolve any epidemics, discard to 7 cards) 3. Infect cities." The available actions include:

  • Drive/Ferry: move to a city connected by a line on the map.
  • Direct Flight: discard a City card to move to the city named on that card.
  • Charter Flight: discard the City card matching your current city to move anywhere.
  • Shuttle Flight: move between any two cities that both already have research stations.
  • Build a Research Station: discard the City card matching your current city.
  • Treat Disease: remove one disease cube from your current city (removing all cubes of a color at once if that disease has already been cured).
  • Share Knowledge: give or take a City card matching your current city, with another player who is also in that city.
  • Discover a Cure: discard 5 matching City cards (4 for the Scientist) at a research station.

Why explaining the structure this way helps new players

New players coming from competitive games often instinctively hide information or avoid asking for help, habits that actively hurt a cooperative game. Framing the explanation around "we all win together or we all lose together," before explaining any specific action, sets the right expectation: full information-sharing between players about their hand of cards, their location, and their plan is not just allowed, it's necessary, since the rulebook's own loss conditions (running out of cards, cubes, or outbreak tolerance) are shared resources that only get managed well with open coordination.

A short way to introduce the game

  1. State the shared win/lose condition first, before any specific rule: "We all win if we cure all four diseases. We all lose if any of three specific bad things happens too many times."
  2. Explain the three loss conditions by name, so the group knows what they're collectively watching for.
  3. Walk through the four core actions (move, treat disease, build a station, share knowledge) since these cover most turns.
  4. Explain Discover a Cure last, since it's the actual win condition and benefits from players already understanding how resources (City cards) accumulate through the other actions first.

Key takeaways

  • A cooperative game's defining trait, stated directly in Pandemic's own rules, is that players win or lose together with no individual scoring.
  • Pandemic has three separate, simultaneous loss conditions (outbreaks, disease cubes, player cards), which is what creates tension despite full cooperation.
  • The Scientist role's documented advantage (4 cards instead of 5 to cure) is a good example of how cooperative games assign complementary, not competing, abilities.
  • Teaching a cooperative game benefits from explaining the shared win/lose condition before any individual action, since it reframes how players are expected to communicate.
